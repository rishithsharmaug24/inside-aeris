How it works Try the forecast

Research forecast · issues no warnings

Step 1 · The question

Will this river cross its flood line in the next two days?

Every evening, for each of 190 river gauges, AERIS gives one number: the chance that a river now below its flood line rises above it tomorrow or the day after.

Why this way

  • The flood line is the 2-year flood flow: the middle value of the yearly peaks at that gauge, worked out from the training years only. Each river gets its own line, and it is crossed about once every two years, often enough to learn from.
  • We forecast the crossing, not "is it in flood". A river already in flood is easy to call. The useful warning comes before the rise.
  • Two days is the main target fixed in the protocol. One and three days were built too; 3 to 7 days is on the road map.
  • A probability, not yes or no, so whoever acts on it can weigh the chance against the cost of acting.

Step 2 · Where

190 river gauges in 15 basins of Peninsular India

Each dot is a Central Water Commission gauge with decades of daily flow records. Thirteen basins were used to build the model. Two whole basins, the Krishna and the east-flowing rivers south of the Pennar, were kept out to test it on rivers it had never seen.

Why this way

  • Open, long, checked records. CAMELS-IND gives daily flow and catchment data from 1980 to 2020 for these rivers. Flow records for the Ganga and Brahmaputra systems are classified, so northern India cannot be tested with open data.
  • Held-out basins answer the question an agency will ask first: does it work on a river it has not learned?
  • One model for all gauges. It learns what rivers have in common, and uses each catchment's description for what differs.

Step 3 · The data

Five kinds of information, each dated to when it was known

A forecast issued on day t may use only what existed by then. Every input carries a date stamp, and tests in the code fail if any input comes from later.

  • Leakage is the most common way flood models look better than they are. Date stamps make it testable instead of a promise.

Step 4 · Choosing inputs

Inputs were chosen by testing them, not by guessing

We added one group of inputs at a time and measured the gain on years the model did not train on. Each group had to earn its place.

Why this way

  • The order is also a check. Rain that really fell beats the river reading, which beats neither. If a model did better without the river reading, something would be wrong.
  • Some attributes were banned. A few catchment numbers shipped with the dataset are computed from the whole flow record, test years included, and would leak the answer.
  • The live version drops ERA5-Land. It arrives 5 days to 3 months late. A GRU without it (G2-nolate: rain, river reading, forecast rain) was level with G2 on validation (−0.006).
  • The gap to 0.241 says better rain forecasts are worth more than any change of model.

The score

Brier score = average of (forecast − outcome)², where the outcome is 1 for a flood and 0 for none. Skill = 1 − (model's Brier ÷ Brier of the usual chance for that day of the year).

Source: reports/v2_report_draft.md §5 · reports/v2_late_inputs.md · src/aeris/features/track_a.py (FORBIDDEN_ATTRIBUTE_CLASSES)

What to say

"We didn't just throw every number in. We added groups one at a time and kept what helped. Adding today's river reading helped, adding the rain forecast helped more, and the neural network got the most out of them. The last bar shows what we could get with a perfect rain forecast, so the rain forecast is the biggest thing to improve."

Step 5 · Preparing the inputs

Putting every river and every storm on the same scale

A neural network learns best when its inputs are of similar size. Daily catchment rain runs from 0 to 432 mm, and the flood flow of a gauge ranges from 39 to 31,807 cubic metres a second, so each input is reshaped first.

Why this way

  • Log scale: on it, 10 mm and 100 mm are one step apart instead of 100 mm dwarfing everything.
  • Dividing by the gauge's own flood flow lets one model serve a stream and a large river: 1.0 always means "at the flood line".
  • Training days only. Scaling with validation or test days would leak information about them.
  • A missing-reading flag lets the network tell "river was low" from "no one read the gauge".

Step 6 · Splitting time

Trained on the past, tested on the future

Rivers remember, so neighbouring days are alike. Splitting days at random would put near-copies of test days into training. We split by whole years, left gaps, and sealed the test years until the very end.

Why this way

  • Validation years chose the settings, the calibration and the alert level. So they cannot also be the evidence.
  • The test years were sealed. Every read was logged, and the code refused to read them before the protocol was frozen.
  • A fresh test came after: 17 months the model never saw, with river data from a different source (CWC via India-WRIS).

Step 7 · Choosing the network

Why a recurrent neural network, and why a GRU

Whether today's rain becomes a flood depends on the weeks before: wet soil and full channels send rain straight to the river. A recurrent network reads the days in order and carries a memory forward, much as a catchment stores water.

Why this way

  • LSTM is the established network for rain and river flow. A GRU does the same job with two gates instead of three, so its memory layer has a quarter fewer weights and trains faster (59 s against 83 s per run in v1).
  • In v1 the GRU scored highest of 13 model types, so it was carried into v2.
  • Convolution (TCN) and attention (Transformer) did worse here, with far more weights.

Fair to say: the GRU's lead over the best tree model was not proven in v1 (+0.015, interval −0.005 to +0.040). On the v2 test the tree model L2 scored the same as the GRU (+0.209 against +0.210). The GRU was named the headline model before the test because it had the best validation score.

Source: reports/model_comparison.md §1.3, §1.5 · docs/F_model_comparison_plan.md §2.3

What to say

"Rivers have memory. If it rained all week, the same storm floods more. A recurrent network reads 180 days in order and keeps a memory, a bit like the soil storing water. We compared 13 kinds of model and the GRU came out on top on the tuning years."

Step 8 · The architecture

The network, layer by layer: 27,185 learned numbers

Two kinds of input go in: 180 days of daily readings, and one list of facts about the catchment and the coming rain. Out comes one probability.

Why this way

  • The catchment facts go in twice: on every day, so the memory knows what kind of river it is reading, and again at the end, so the final judgement can use them directly.
  • Only the last memory state is used. By day 180 it holds whatever the network learned to keep.
  • Small on purpose: 27 thousand weights against 783 thousand training rows with only 3,785 floods.

The 15 daily channels (G2)

Rain (log), rain ÷ the catchment's 99th-percentile day, maximum and minimum temperature, humidity, wind, shortwave and longwave sunshine, potential evaporation, soil moisture in 4 layers, river flow ÷ flood flow (log), and a missing-reading flag. The live version, G2-nolate, keeps only rain, rain ÷ 99th percentile, river flow and the flag: 4 channels, 25,073 weights.

Source: src/aeris/models/sequence.py (_build_net) · run.json model_complexity

What to say

"Here is the whole network. 180 days of readings go into the GRU, which keeps a memory of 64 numbers. The catchment description is added on every day and again at the end. Two small layers turn that into one probability. In total it learns about 27 thousand numbers."

Step 9 · Inside one step

Inside a GRU step: keep, reset, write

Each day the GRU takes that day's inputs x and yesterday's memory h. Two gates, each a number between 0 and 1, decide how much memory to keep and what new content to write.

Why this way

  • The last line is the trick. When the keep gate z is near 1, memory passes through almost unchanged, and so does the training signal, across all 180 days.
  • A plain recurrent network multiplies its memory by the same weights every day. Over 180 days the signal shrinks to nothing or blows up. Gates fix that.
  • An LSTM has a third gate and a separate cell memory. The GRU merges them, and here that cost no skill.

Step 10 · Activation functions

Activation functions, and why each one sits where it does

An activation function bends a layer's output, so the network can learn curved relationships such as "a little rain does nothing, a lot does a great deal". AERIS uses three.

FunctionOutputWhere, and why
Sigmoid
1 ÷ (1 + e−x)		0 to 1The GRU gates, because a gate is a fraction: how much to let through. The final output, because a chance must lie between 0 and 1.
tanh−1 to 1The GRU's candidate memory: centred on zero, so memory can move up or down, and bounded, so it cannot run away over 180 days. Also the catchment embedding, to keep it on the same scale as the scaled daily inputs.
ReLU
max(0, x)		0 or moreThe 64-unit layer of the head: simple and fast, and its slope is 1 for any positive input, so the training signal passes through without shrinking.

Why this way

  • Without activations, stacked layers collapse into one straight-line model, no better than logistic regression.
  • Slope matters for learning. Sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, tanh's at most 1. That is one reason tanh, not sigmoid, carries the memory.

Fair to say: the gate and memory functions come with the GRU's design, and ReLU and tanh in the other layers are the standard choices. The activations were not tuned. The search tuned window length, memory size, dropout and learning rate instead (step 13).

Source: src/aeris/models/sequence.py (_build_net)

What to say

"Activation functions make the network non-linear. We use sigmoid where we need a fraction or a probability, tanh for the memory because it stays balanced and bounded, and ReLU in the final layer because it is simple and trains well."

Step 11 · The loss function

How the network is marked: log loss

Training needs one number that says how wrong the forecasts are. AERIS uses log loss, also called binary cross-entropy. A confident forecast that turns out wrong is punished very hard.

L = −[ y·ln(p) + (1 − y)·ln(1 − p) ] averaged over 1,024 examples

Why this way

  • A proper scoring rule. The forecast that minimises log loss is the true probability, so the network is rewarded for honest chances, not bold ones.
  • Floods were not up-weighted. They are 0.48% of training days. Weighting them up would push every forecast too high, and "20%" would stop meaning 20%.
  • It starts at the base rate. The output bias is set to ln(0.0048 ÷ 0.9952) ≈ −5.3, so on step 1 the network already says 0.48%, not 50%.
  • Settings and stopping are judged by the Brier score, another proper score and the headline measure.

Source: src/aeris/models/sequence.py fit(): BCEWithLogitsLoss, bias set from the base rate

What to say

"To learn, the network needs a mark for every forecast. Log loss punishes being confidently wrong. We did not boost the rare flood days, because we want a 20 percent forecast to really mean 20 percent."

Step 12 · Training

The training loop: up to 12,000 steps on a laptop GPU

Each step shows the network 1,024 random gauge-days, measures its log loss, and nudges all 27,185 weights to lower it. Every 1,000 steps it is checked on the validation years and the best version is kept.

Why this way

  • AdamW adapts the step size for each weight (learning rate 0.0003), and weight decay (0.00001) pulls weights gently toward zero to limit overfitting.
  • Gradient clipping to length 1 stops the occasional huge gradient that recurrent networks produce from wrecking the weights.
  • Early stopping is the main guard against memorising. The kept versions came from steps 9,000, 6,000 and 5,000.
  • 16-bit arithmetic on the GPU saves memory and time on the 8 GB laptop card.

Step 13 · Tuning

Twelve settings tried, one kept

Some choices are not learned in training: how many days to read, how big the memory is, how much dropout, how fast to learn. We fixed a grid of 81 combinations, tried 12, and kept the one with the lowest validation Brier score.

SettingChoicesKept
Days read (window)90 · 180 · 365180
Memory units64 · 128 · 25664
Dropout0 · 0.2 · 0.40
Learning rate0.0003 · 0.001 · 0.0030.0003

Why this way

  • The smallest memory won, with the slowest learning rate. With few floods to learn from, a small network generalises better than a big one.
  • 180 days beat 365. Half a year covers the monsoon build-up. The 365-day, 256-unit trial took 712 s and scored worse.
  • The test years played no part. The search used validation years and one seed.
  • The same 12 settings were tried for G2-nolate, and the same one won.

Step 14 · Finishing

Three seeds, a calibration check and an alert level

The chosen setting was trained three times from different random starts, and the three forecasts are averaged. Then two last questions: are the probabilities honest, and where should an alert begin?

Why this way

  • Averaging seeds smooths out the luck of one run. Their forecasts differed by a spread of 0.0009 in probability on average.
  • Calibration was tested, not assumed. Two standard corrections were tried against none; none won, because log loss and the base-rate start had already made the probabilities honest.
  • The alert level is 6.3%: the probability above which only 1% of validation days without a flood were alerted. It was fixed before the test and never changed.

Step 15 · The rules

A test the model was allowed to fail

Before anyone looked at the test years, the rules were written down and frozen: which models, which gauges, which years, and what would count as better.

Why this way

  • It stops the most common error in forecasting studies: trying things on the test until something wins.
  • Intervals come from resampling whole sub-basins and whole years, 500 times, because the days of one flood are not independent.
  • Sanity checks passed. With shuffled labels the model scores 0.0005 against a limit of 0.005, as it should. 159 automated tests pass.

Source: docs/G2_protocol_v2.md · catalog/protocol_v2_freeze.json · catalog/fresh_test_wris_freeze.json · reports/v2_report_draft.md §8

What to say

"We wrote the rules of the exam before the exam. The test years were locked, and we opened them once. If the model had failed, we would have had to report that."

Step 17 · Limits and next steps

What it cannot do yet

AERIS is a research forecast, tested on past years with archived data. It issues no warnings.

Limits we measured

  • False alarms are frequent: about 3 in 4 alert episodes had no flood, and in wet years the alert level raises more.
  • Weaker on rivers it never saw: positive but not proven.
  • Short warning: 1–2 days, where evacuations often need 3–7.
  • River records disagree: in 2020, GUARDIAN and CWC flows agreed within 10% on only 19% of days.
  • No live river feed: the open CWC flow record runs months behind.

Source: reports/v2_report_draft.md §11–12 · docs/J_roadmap.md §5

What to say

"We are clear about the limits. It is a research forecast. To be used by an agency it needs a full live season, longer warnings, fewer false alarms and an independent review. Condition one is partly met; the rest is our road map."
